Today I’ll present my dissertation, “From chromatin footprints to gene regulatory networks.”

The central goal of this work is to develop reproducible methods that move from open chromatin, to TF-binding prediction, and finally to link target genes.

I’ll start with a simple view of transcriptional regulation. When chromatin is open, transcription factors can bind DNA and help recruit RNA polymerase II, which initiates transcription. This brings us to two main questions: where do transcription factors bind, and which genes do they regulate?

Let’s start with the first question: where do TFs bind?

First, the DNA needs to be accessible. Then a transcription factor can bind.

But TFs do not bind DNA randomly. They recognize short DNA sequence patterns called motifs, like the sequence logo shown here.

Different TFs prefer different motifs. For example, HNF4A, GATA, SOX, and AP-1 each have their own sequence preference.

These known motifs are collected in databases such as JASPAR and HOCOMOCO.

So motifs tell us where a TF could bind, but not whether it is actually bound.

But there is another problem: different TFs can recognize very similar motifs.

Here, I took the JASPAR motifs and grouped them by sequence similarity.

In the center is the full motif collection, and around the outside you can see that similar motifs naturally form families, such as Forkhead, Homeobox, and bHLH, and other TF families

The important point is that TFs within the same family can recognize very similar DNA sequences.

So a motif may tell us the TF family, but not necessarily the exact TF that is bound.

To identify the specific TF, we need TF-specific experimental measurements

Here are three common TF-specific assays.

On the left, ChIP-seq uses an antibody to pull down DNA associated with the TF.

In the middle, CUT&RUN uses the antibody to recruit a nuclease and cut nearby DNA.

On the right, CUT&Tag uses the antibody to recruit Tn5 and cut and tag nearby DNA.

Different mechanisms, but the same goal: after sequencing, we identify where that TF is bound.

These assays give us much stronger TF-binding evidence than motif matching alone

The problem is scale.

We have many TFs, but these are targeted assays, so each TF usually needs its own experiment.

That quickly becomes many experiments, and it also depends on having a good TF-specific antibody.

More experiments means more time and cost.

So we need a more scalable way to study TF binding.

That brings us to ATAC-seq.

ATAC-seq tells us where the chromatin is open.

First, we have closed and open chromatin.

Second, Tn5 inserts into the accessible DNA.

After sequencing, those insertions build up into ATAC-seq peaks.

So a high ATAC-seq signal means the chromatin is more accessible.

But if we look inside that peak more closely, the local Tn5 pattern can tell us more about TF binding.

ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing)

Here is the basic idea of ATAC-seq footprinting.

Tn5 can cut the accessible DNA on both sides of the site.

But when a TF is bound in the middle, it protects that DNA, so we see fewer cuts in the center.

That dip in the middle is the footprint.

And on the right, the AP-1 example shows the same pattern in real data — high signal on the sides, and a dip at the motif center.

So footprinting gives us more localized evidence of TF binding than the broad ATAC-seq peak alone.

CraftGRN Module 1 asks the next question: which TF is most likely responsible for each footprint?

But assigning a TF to a footprint is not straightforward, because TF binding is highly dependent on biological context.

The same genomic site can be occupied in one cell type but not another. And even within the same cell type, binding can change between biological conditions. So TF binding is dynamic and cell-type specific.

A static sequence motif alone cannot capture that context.

And context is only one part of the problem. The DNA sequences recognized by TFs are also more flexible than a single canonical motif.

A TF may have a preferred canonical motif, but real binding is more complicated.

TFs can recognize motif variants, bind cooperatively with another TF at composite sites, or be recruited indirectly through tethering without contacting their own motif.

So canonical motif matching alone cannot explain every TF-binding event.

We can see that limitation directly in our HNF4A data.

Here I’m using HNF4A in HPAF-II pancreatic cancer cells as a representative example.

The purple signal is the independent HNF4A CUT&Tag measurement, the purple bars mark called peaks, and the orange bars mark canonical HNF4A motif matches.

Several strong HNF4A peaks do not contain a canonical motif match.

This shows that motif information explains only part of the observed HNF4A binding landscape.

There are three main computational approaches to TF-binding prediction.

First, motif-based methods like FIMO or HOMER search for known TF sequence motifs and then treat motif matching as TF binding.

Second, footprinting methods like TOBIAS and HINT-ATAC treat footprint + motif matching as TF binding.

Third, supervised models such as EPCOT learn from existing TF-binding data. EPCOT uses DNA sequence and cell-type-specific ATAC to predict TF binding. It can generalize to new cell types, but only for the TFs learned by the pretrained model, and its predictions are at one-kilobase resolution, rather than individual footprints. And limited to generalize to new cell types.

So what we still need is a way to assign candidate TFs directly at footprint resolution, using the biological context of our own samples.

That is the problem addressed by CraftGRN Module 1.

Module 1 uses matched ATAC-seq and RNA-seq across the same biological conditions.

We begin with a footprint and use motif information to define plausible TF candidates. We then compare the footprint activity with each candidate TF’s expression across conditions.

When TF expression and footprint activity vary together, that relationship provides condition-specific support for the TF–footprint pair. When they do not vary together, the pair is not retained.

For supported footprints, we can then expand the search to the broader set of expressed TFs.

Before predicting TF binding, we first remove redundancy.

Different motif annotations can produce several overlapping calls over the same underlying footprint. Treating all of those calls as independent would overcount the region.

CraftGRN therefore merges overlapping calls into a single nonredundant footprint, while retaining the original motif-family information for downstream analysis.

Here I compare the same number of top-ranked footprints before and after consolidation.

Before consolidation, many top calls are repeated observations from the same motif or TF families. After consolidation, those repeated calls are reduced.

As a result, the top footprint set represents a broader and less redundant collection of TF families.

This gives us a cleaner starting point for TF-binding prediction.

Before showing the comparison, I’ll briefly define the main metrics.

Recall, or true-positive rate, is the fraction of experimentally detected binding sites that we recover. Precision is the fraction of our predictions that are experimentally supported. The false-positive rate measures how often we incorrectly predict binding at negative sites.

CraftGRN Module 2 asks the next question: which gene is that TF likely to regulate?

A predicted TF-binding site does not automatically identify its target gene.

Experimentally, there are two major types of evidence we can use.

One is TF perturbation followed by RNA-seq. We knock down, knock out, or overexpress a TF and look for genes that change. But these experiments are time-consuming and costly, and the expression changes can include both direct and indirect effects. A real target can also be missed if regulation depends on multiple TFs, if another TF compensates, or if we measure at the wrong time point.

The other is 3D chromatin interaction data, such as Hi-C or HiChIP. These assays can provide evidence of physical contact, but they are also resource-intensive, their resolution can be broad, and physical contact does not necessarily imply functional regulation.

That is why computational methods combine different types of evidence to predict target genes are needed.

Computationally, there are several common strategies.

The simplest is proximity—for example, linking a binding site to the nearest gene or to genes within a TSS window. This is easy to apply, but it can miss distal regulation.

A second approach uses 3D contact information or regulatory priors. Hi-C and HiChIP provide physical-contact information, while models such as ABC and ENCODE-rE2G combine multiple features to predict enhancer–gene relationships.

Co-accessibility approaches ask whether regulatory regions and promoters show coordinated chromatin changes across cells or samples.

Finally, Integrated models such as Taiji, GRaNIE, and SCENIC+ combine accessibility, expression, motif, and other regulatory evidence.

The important point is that these approaches use different types of evidence, and no single strategy is ideal for every dataset.

CraftGRN Module 2 follows the same idea: use the predicted TF-binding sites from Module 1, then combine genomic distance with matched multi-omic evidence and optional regulatory priors to identify likely target genes

Back up: integrated models combine multiple data types. For example, Taiji uses chromatin accessibility, motif and gene expression to build regulatory networks; GRaNIE integrates open chromatin with RNA-seq; and SCENIC+ combines single-cell chromatin accessibility, gene expression, and motif-based regulatory information.

So rather than relying on only one type of evidence, CraftGRN combines the information available in our matched datasets

Module 2 begins with the TF-binding sites predicted by Module 1.

For each site, we identify nearby candidate genes within a genomic window. If three-dimensional interaction information is available, it can be used as an additional prior.

Across matched biological conditions, we then ask two questions: does target-gene expression track with TF expression, and does it also track with footprint activity?

When both relationships provide support, the TF–binding-site–gene relationship is retained as a predicted TF–target gene link.

I’ll now summarize the main contributions of the dissertation.

To summarize, this work makes three connected contributions.

First, fp-tools provides reproducible and efficient ATAC-seq footprinting for bulk and single-cell data.

Second, CraftGRN Module 1 combines footprints, motifs, and TF expression to predict condition-specific TF-binding sites.

Third, CraftGRN Module 2 combines genomic proximity with matched regulatory evidence to predict likely target genes.

Independent TF-binding and perturbation datasets support these predictions across pancreatic cancer and T-cell systems.

Overall, fp-tools and CraftGRN provide an integrated path from chromatin accessibility, to TF binding, and finally to predicted gene regulation.

These results also point to several future directions.

There are three main directions for extending this work.

First, because footprint performance depends on the TF, future versions could select or combine footprint callers based on TF-specific performance.

Second, CraftGRN can be extended from condition-level analysis to cell-state-resolved networks using single-cell multiome data.

Third, Module 3 will focus on differential regulatory programs—identifying coordinated TF–gene programs that change across biological conditions.

Improved computational efficiency
Faster execution and lower peak memory in the matched benchmark.

De novo motif discovery integrated with footprint analysis
Candidate footprints can be exported, analyzed with MEME/DREME/STREME, compared with known motifs using Tomtom, and carried back into downstream analysis.

Biological-replicate differential footprinting
Per-replicate motif scores, replicate summaries, empirical-Bayes moderated testing, and multiple-testing correction rather than treating conditions as single profiles.

Cross-sample footprint normalization and scaled aggregation
Including shared-background q95 scaling for multi-sample comparisons.

Integrated pseudobulk single-cell workflow
Grouped scATAC fragments can enter the same core footprint pipeline, with cell-level KNN-smoothed footprint signatures for visualization.

Interactive and publication-oriented reporting
Interactive HTML reports, linked motif/footprint views, and editable SVG export.

Reproducible execution surfaces
Command line, saved YAML configurations, browser GUI, run history, and auditable analysis settings.

Orthogonal validation framework
The full workflow was benchmarked against matched TF ChIP-seq across 12 TF–cell-line comparisons in five cancer cell lines; footprint-score AUROC exceeded PWM AUROC in 10 of 12 comparisons.
