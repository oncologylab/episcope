Today I’ll present my dissertation, “From chromatin footprints to gene regulatory networks.”

The central goal of this work is to develop reproducible methods that move from open chromatin, to TF-binding prediction, and finally to link target genes.

As everyone in this room know, transcription is a fundamental process in biology. Just a quick reminder: transcription happens at open chromatin. It starts with transcription factors binding to enhancers or promoters and help recruit co-activators and ultimately RNA polymerase to initiate transcription.

But TFs do not bind DNA randomly. They recognize different short DNA sequence patterns called motifs such as shown in here. You can appreciate here that Different types of TFs prefer different sequences to bind. There are public database such as JASPAR and HOCOMOCO that collect thousands of known motifs that have already been discovered. However, a lot of TFs binding patterns are still unknown.

On the other hand, many different TFs can recognize very similar motifs as shown in from the JASPAR collection. I highlighted some of the large TF families, which can contain dozens of members.

To make the story even more complex, TFs do not always bind to their canonical motifs. It is well known that TFs can bind slightly different sequences called motif variants, or they can bind cooperatively with other TFs to create new binding sites called composite sites, Alternatively, they can be recruited by other TFs to completely different genomic loci.

Furthermore, TF binding is cell type specific and highly dynamic. The same TFs can bind to completely different genomic regions in different cell types. And even within the same cell type, their binding can change between biological conditions. Therefore, a static motif matching cannot predict the actual TF binding.

As a result, people have developed many different techniques to detect actual TF binding. Here are three major assays, ChIP-seq, CUT&Run and CUT&Tag, all requiring specific antibodies to recognize the TFs. While ChIP-seq involves crosslinking and pulldown of fragmented DNA, CUT&Run and CUT&Tag use nuclease to cut native DNA near where TFs bind. While these techniques are fundamental in helping us understand how TF bind to DNA and regulate transcription, they are technically challenging and require high quality antibodies that are not available for many TFs. They are time-consuming, labor intensive and expensive, and you can only study one TF at a time.

So we need a more scalable way to study TF binding.

That brings us to ATAC-seq.

ATAC-seq take advantage of the simple fact that transcription happens at open chromatin where DNA is not protected by the nucleosomes.

It uses Tn5 transposes to cut and insert specific primers into these easily accessible genomic regions

Then it uses PCR to amply out these open chromatin regions.

These ampllied fragments are subjected to HTS, mapped back to the reference genome, allowing us to identify highly accessible regions represented as peaks.

But there is a twist. Within the open chromatin region, if a TF is bound, it protects the exact site where it binds from Tn5 cutting, causing a dip in the middle of the Peak, almost look like a footprint.

Here is an actual example of the aggregated footprints around JUN? motifs across the genomic from ATAC analysis of HPAFII? Cells .

You can appreciate the high signal on either side of the motif, but a clear drop in signal in the motif center, indicating strong binding at these sites.

In contrast, no dips are detected at the ## motif sites, suggesting little binding by this TF in this cell line.

You can actually compute a footprint by dividing the flank signals to the depth of the footprints, which reflect the relative binding strength of each TFs.

As a result, from a single ATAC experiment, you can predict global TF binding across the genome, making it a very simple yet powerful technique that is widely used by many labs.

A number of tools are already available to perform footprinting. However, they each have their drawbacks as listed here.

Therefore, we set out to develop a new framework called fp-tools to extend existing methods to support de novo motif discovery, biological-replicate analysis, single-cell workflows, and improved computational efficiency.

Furthermore, we extended another package called CraftGRN that goes beyond basic footrpints and predict TF-binding sites and target genes from paired ATAC and RNAseq data.

Now I will introduce CraftGRN.

As I explained earlier, TF binding is dynamic and cell-type specific.

TFs bind to both canonical and non-canonical sites.

Here is an example showing detected HNF4A binding sites in HPAF-II pancreatic cancer cells using Cut&Tag.

As you can see, the majority of the HNF4A binding sites do not contain canonical HNF4A binding motifs.

Thus, motif/footprint alone are not sufficient for predicting actual TF binding sites.

To address this issue, several supervised learning methods such as EPCOT have been developed to predict TF binding using existing TF-binding data. E

However, because as I mentioned earlier, many TFs do not have published binding data. Even for those that have public binding data, they are usually limited to a few cell lines. Thus, these supervised learning methods can only predict TF bindings on on limited numbers of TFs and have to generalize TF binding to new cell types that are plagued with inaccuracies.

Because the computational intensive nature of these methods, these methods can only predict at around one-kilobase resolution, rather than at precise footprint level.

Finally, these methods are so complex that only a skilled bioinformatician lilke me with access to high end server is able to implement them.

Instead of relying on existing mismatched data, CraftGRN uses matched ATAC-seq and RNA-seq across the same biological conditions.

First, we use fp-tools to process ATAC-seq data to detect and score all the footprints across the genome.

We then compare the footprint activity with each TF’s expression across conditions, under the assumption that if a TF truly binds to a specific genomic locus, its binding activities should correlate with its expression fluctuation across conditions.

However, one of the technical issue we faced is that different TFs can recognize very similar motifs.

As a result, the same footprint region is often matched to many TF motifs, and there is huge variation between regions with some regions matching to many TFs, others only matching to only a single motif.

However, it is also true some TFs bind next to each other in separate motifs, so we also have to take care to not merge motifs that are functional distinct.

By utilizing motif scores as a guide, we were able to develop an optimized strategy that collapse truly redundant footprints.

Here I compare the top 100 differential footproints before and after collapsing.

As you can see, Before consolidation, the top TFs were dominated by a handful of large TF families.

After consolidation, a much broader collection of TF families are represented.

This correction thus significantly reduced bias caused by TF redundancy.

EPCOT can benefit from GPU acceleration, but its outputs remain limited to the TFs supported by the pretrained model.

So Module 1 combines broader TF coverage, lower computational cost, and competitive or improved prediction performance.

Module 1 tells us where a TF is likely to bind.

CraftGRN Module 2 asks the next question: which genes are that TF likely to regulate from the genomic loci?

Experimentally, there are two major types of evidence that people generally use to identify target genes.

One is TF perturbation followed by RNA-seq. We knock down, knock out, or overexpress a TF and look for genes that change. But these experiments are time-consuming and costly, and the expression changes can include both direct and indirect effects. A real target can also be missed if regulation depends on multiple TFs, if another TF compensates, or if we measure at the wrong time point.

The other is 3D chromatin interaction data, such as Hi-C or HiChIP. These assays can provide evidence of physical contact, but they are also resource-intensive, their resolution can be generally very low (often ~5kb), and physical contacts do not necessarily imply functional regulation.

That is why computational methods combine different types of evidence to predict target genes are needed.

Computationally, there are several common strategies.

The simplest is proximity—for example, linking a binding site to the nearest gene or to genes within a TSS window. This is easy to apply, but it is often wrong as Enhancers don’t always regulate the closest genes.

A second approach such as ABC and ENCODE-rE2G uses 3D contact information or regulatory priors. Basically combining Hi-C and HiChIP physical-contact information with RNAseq to predict enhancer–gene relationships.

A third Co-accessibility approach performs under the assumption that enhancers and promoters show coordinated chromatin changes across cells or samples should regulate each other.

Finally, Integrated models such as Taiji, GRaNIE, and SCENIC+ combine accessibility, expression, motif, and other regulatory evidence.

However, our experiences in implementing these methods have left us disappointed with their inaccuracies.

We therefore set out to develop our own method.

Module 2 begins with the TF-binding sites predicted by Module 1.

For each site, we identify nearby candidate genes within a user-defined genomic window. If three-dimensional interaction information is available, it can be used as an additional prior.

Across matched biological conditions, we then ask two questions: does target-gene expression track with TF expression, and does it also track with the footprint activities for that TF?

When both relationships provide support, the TF–binding-site–gene relationship is retained as a predicted TF–target gene link.

We then evaluated whether these predicted targets respond in independent TF-perturbation experiments.

Again without boring you through the series of optimization steps we performed, here is the final benchmarking results comparing CraftGRN to two state-of-art target gene prediction methods, Tajji and GRaNIE.

In contrast to the low precision rates of these two existing methods, CraftGRN in green is able to identify experimentally supported target genes for SOX9 and AsPC1 in two different PDAC cell lines.

Importantly, the target gene confidence score calculated by CraftGRN tracks with the magnitude of log2 fold change from genetic silencing as determined by actual RNAseq, with top 100 scored predicted target genes showing more average expression decrease compared to top 1000 scored target genes.

Panels C and D summarize the predicted networks. The central red node is SOX9 or KLF5, the blue boxes are additional TFs, and the gray circles are genes.

Importantly, CraftGRN not only accurately predicted target genes for individual TFs, it predicts target genes for all expressed TFs, thus allowing us to build gene regulatory networks.

By building these GRNs, we are able to find other TFs that work together with SOX9 or KLF5 in co-regulating these target genes, thus identifying biologically meaning regulatory edges for further investigation.

So for the experimental biologists in the room, CraftGRN can not only help you generate a list of potential target genes to validate for your favorite TF, it can also help you find its friends and collaborators, basically giving you clues on other relevant TFs you might want to consider.

To summarize, this work makes three connected contributions.

First, fp-tools provides reproducible and efficient ATAC-seq footprinting for bulk and single-cell data.

Second, CraftGRN Module 1 combines footprints, motifs, and TF expression to predict condition-specific TF-binding sites.

Third, CraftGRN Module 2 combines genomic proximity with matched regulatory evidence to predict likely target genes.

Independent TF-binding and perturbation datasets support these predictions across pancreatic cancer and T-cell systems.

Overall, fp-tools and CraftGRN provide an integrated path from chromatin accessibility, to TF binding, and finally to predicted gene regulation.

These results also point to several future directions.

There are 2 main directions for extending this work.

First, we are in active development of CraftGRN Module 3, which focuses on automating the extraction of biologically meaningful GRNs and performing differential GRN analysis across conditions.

Second, we want to extend CraftGRN from condition-level analysis to cell-state-resolved networks using single-cell multiome data, and ultimately apply to spatial single-cell data.
